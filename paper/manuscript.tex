\begin{abstract}
Evaluating factual faithfulness in cross-lingual Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) poses a fundamental dilemma in low-resource settings: should automated evaluators score English generations directly against native-language retrieved evidence (Condition $C1$), or evaluate monolingually following machine translation into English (Condition $C2$)? 
In this paper, we present \textbf{NepFaith-120}, the first causal error decomposition benchmark decoupling machine translation error ($\Delta_{\text{MT}}$) from cross-lingual evaluation bottlenecks ($\Delta_{\text{XL}}$) using a minimal-pair human post-edited translation ceiling (Condition $C3$). 
Covering $n=120$ claim-level, doubly adjudicated items across Nepali Wikipedia, Government Reports, and Contemporary News (with a 30\% fine-grained sports quota), alongside an $N=1,200$ unlabelled inference corpus under Prediction-Powered Inference (PPI++), we benchmark five automated evaluator architectures. 
Our empirical findings show that frontier judges (\texttt{gemini-3.6-flash}) achieve high native agreement with human gold labels on Nepali context ($91.67\%$ exact match, $A(C1) = 56.7 \pm 8.9\%$). However, performance collapses by 52.5 percentage points under raw machine translation ($A(C2) = 4.2 \pm 3.6\%$). Human post-editing restores faithfulness to $31.7 \pm 8.4\%$, demonstrating that machine translation noise dominates over cross-lingual reasoning limitations ($\Delta_{\text{MT}} = +27.5\%$, $\Delta_{\text{XL}} = -25.0\%$). 
Furthermore, cluster-robust PPI++ estimation demonstrates sample efficiency gains of $1.15\times$ to $1.73\times$, providing provably valid coverage guarantees for low-resource NLP evaluation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is increasingly deployed in non-English information spaces, retrieving local-language documentation to synthesize structured answers for global audiences. However, assessing whether the generated response is factually grounded in the retrieved passage presents a fundamental architectural fork:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Direct Cross-Lingual Evaluation ($C1$):} The automated evaluator scores the English generated claim directly against the verbatim native-language context (e.g., Nepali written in Devanagari script).
    \item \textbf{Translate-then-Evaluate ($C2$):} The native context is translated into English via neural machine translation (e.g., \texttt{IndicTrans2} \cite{gala2023indictrans2}), followed by monolingual English verification.
\end{enumerate}

Conventional wisdom in natural language processing assumes that machine translation introduces severe semantic distortion, hallucination, and omission, fatally compromising downstream evaluation. Conversely, cross-lingual evaluators themselves struggle with semantic entailment over low-resource scripts that are underrepresented in multilingual training corpora. This tension yields a critical scientific question: \textit{Does cross-lingual faithfulness evaluation fail primarily because machine translation corrupts the source context ($\Delta_{\text{MT}}$), or because automated evaluators fail at cross-lingual alignment ($\Delta_{\text{XL}}$)?}

To answer this question definitively, we introduce \textbf{NepFaith-120}, a benchmark designed around a three-condition paired counterfactual framework. By introducing Condition $C3$---a minimal-pair human post-edited machine translation ceiling---we cleanly decouple translation artifacts from evaluator reasoning deficits. 

Nepali represents a critical stress test for cross-lingual NLP: while included in raw multilingual web crawls (such as CC100), it is completely omitted from standard Natural Language Inference benchmarks (e.g., XNLI covers 15 languages excluding Nepali; IndicXNLI covers 11 Indian languages, also excluding Nepali). Furthermore, Nepali exhibits split-ergativity, complex honorific morphology, and distinct Bikram Sambat calendar systems that routinely trip automated systems.

To avoid the prohibitive costs of exhaustive human annotation while preserving rigorous mathematical guarantees, we leverage Prediction-Powered Inference (PPI++) \cite{angelopoulos2023ppi, gao2026ppsr}. By combining $n=120$ doubly adjudicated gold labels with an unlabelled corpus of $N=1,200$ items, we establish cluster-robust confidence intervals that account for claim clustering and positive paired covariance.

The contributions of this work are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Causal Error Decomposition:} We formalize the first counterfactual framework decoupling machine translation error ($\Delta_{\text{MT}}$) from cross-lingual evaluation bottlenecks ($\Delta_{\text{XL}}$) via a human post-edited translation ceiling ($C3$).
    \item \textbf{The NepFaith-120 Benchmark:} A balanced benchmark of $n=120$ doubly adjudicated claims stratified across Nepali Wikipedia, Government Reports, and News, featuring a 7-type perturbation engine (entity swaps, dates, negation, misattribution, register shifts, additions, causalities).
    \item \textbf{Frontier Evaluation Suite:} We benchmark five evaluator arms, showing that while classical embedding and NLI baselines suffer calibration failure, frontier LLMs (\texttt{gemini-3.6-flash}) achieve 91.67\% agreement with human consensus on native Nepali, but drop by 52.5 percentage points under raw machine translation.
    \item \textbf{Rigorous Statistical Scaling:} We apply cluster-robust PPI++ to low-resource faithfulness evaluation, achieving Prediction-Powered Sample-size Ratios (PPSR) up to $1.73\times$ while proving valid 95\% confidence interval coverage.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work \& Positioning}
\label{sec:related}
\subsection{RAG Faithfulness \& Hallucination Metrics}
Monolingual factual consistency evaluators, including RAGAS, TruLens, and HHEM-2.1, evaluate claims strictly in English or rely on uncalibrated zero-shot prompt translation. When applied cross-lingually, existing pipelines treat machine translation as an unexamined utility channel. Multilingual metrics such as mFACT evaluate abstractive summarization via MT but treat MT as a black-box transfer mechanism without isolating $\Delta_{\text{MT}}$ from $\Delta_{\text{XL}}$, and omit Nepali entirely. NepFaith-120 addresses this gap by introducing a controlled human post-edited ceiling ($C3$) to isolate translation noise causally.

\subsection{Symmetric NLI vs. Asymmetric RAG Grounding}
Classical cross-lingual NLI benchmarks evaluate \textit{Translate-Test} against \textit{Zero-Shot Cross-Lingual Transfer}. However, classical NLI is strictly symmetric: premise and hypothesis share the same language. In cross-lingual RAG, evaluation is inherently asymmetric: the retrieved premise is in source language $L_{\text{src}}$ (Nepali), whereas the generated claim is in target language $L_{\text{tgt}}$ (English). Prior NLI comparisons directly subtract Zero-Shot from Translate-Test, conflating translation corruption with semantic reasoning limitations.

\subsection{Indic NLP \& Evaluation Deficits}
Recent multilingual models, such as IndicTrans2 \cite{gala2023indictrans2}, BGE-M3 \cite{chen2024bgem3}, and mDeBERTa-v3 \cite{laurer2022mdeberta}, support diverse Indic languages. However, Nepali remains systematically under-benchmarked. Hallucination suites like MultiWikiQHalluA and BHRAM-IL either truncated Nepali coverage or focused on open-ended generation rather than faithfulness evaluation. NepFaith-120 provides the first dedicated cross-lingual faithfulness benchmark for Nepali.

\subsection{Prediction-Powered Inference in NLP}
Prediction-Powered Inference \cite{angelopoulos2023ppi} enables valid statistical inference by combining a small gold dataset with machine learning predictions over large unlabelled corpora. Gao et al. \cite{gao2026ppsr} demonstrated that neural metrics save 50\%--80\% of human annotation in English NLP benchmarks. In this work, we integrate cluster-robust, paired PPI++ to low-resource cross-lingual evaluation, exploiting positive paired covariance between counterfactual conditions.

\section{Problem Formulation \& Decomposition}
\label{sec:formulation}
Let each evaluation instance consist of an authentic retrieved Nepali passage $p_{\text{ne}}$, a user query $q$, and an English generation $a_{\text{en}}$. The response is segmented into $M_i$ atomic, self-contained factual claims $\{k_{ij}\}_{j=1}^{M_i}$.

Each claim $k_{ij}$ is evaluated under three counterfactual conditions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Condition $C1$ (Native Cross-Lingual):} The evaluator scores the English claim directly against the verbatim Nepali source context:
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{Y}_{ij}^{(C1)} = E(p_{\text{ne}}, k_{ij})
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Condition $C2$ (Raw Machine Translation):} The Nepali passage is translated into English using IndicTrans2 \cite{gala2023indictrans2}, and evaluated monolingually:
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{Y}_{ij}^{(C2)} = E(\text{MT}(p_{\text{ne}}), k_{ij})
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Condition $C3$ (Human Post-Edited Ceiling):} Bilingual human editors minimally correct translation errors in $\text{MT}(p_{\text{ne}})$ while preserving syntactic structure:
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{Y}_{ij}^{(C3)} = E(\text{PE}(\text{MT}(p_{\text{ne}})), k_{ij})
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Causal Decomposition Metrics}
Let $A(C)$ represent the accuracy of an automated evaluator arm against human gold ground truth $Y_{ij}$ under condition $C$. We formally decompose the evaluation error into two orthogonal penalties:
\begin{align}
    \Delta_{\text{MT}} &= A(C3) - A(C2) \quad \text{(MT Translation Noise)} \label{eq:delta_mt} \\
    \Delta_{\text{XL}} &= A(C3) - A(C1) \quad \text{(Cross-Lingual Evaluator Gap)} \label{eq:delta_xl}
\end{align}

Our primary hypothesis investigates whether cross-lingual reasoning failure exceeds machine translation corruption:
\begin{equation}
    H_1: \Delta_{\text{XL}} - \Delta_{\text{MT}} = A(C2) - A(C1)
\end{equation}
A positive difference indicates that cross-lingual reasoning is the primary bottleneck, whereas a negative difference proves that machine translation errors dominate.

\section{The NepFaith-120 Corpus}
\label{sec:corpus}
\subsection{Stratification \& Sampling}
To ensure ecological validity, the NepFaith-120 benchmark is evenly stratified across three macro-domains:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Nepali Wikipedia (33.3\%):} Encyclopedic articles spanning history, geography, and biographies.
    \item \textbf{Government Reports (33.3\%):} Official publications from the National Statistics Office and Nepal Rastra Bank containing dense tabular and numerical data.
    \item \textbf{Contemporary News (33.3\%):} National journalism covering politics, economics, and an explicit 30\% quota of sports reporting (12 items) to test fast-paced entity tracking.
\end{enumerate}

The benchmark comprises an adjudicated gold set of $n=120$ items and an unlabelled inference corpus of $N=1,200$ items, matching the exact domain proportions (400 Wikipedia, 400 Government Reports, 400 News).

\subsection{7-Type Perturbation Engine}
To guarantee an exact 50\%/50\% balance between faithful and unfaithful instances (60 natural faithful, 60 unfaithful in the gold set; 600/600 in the unlabelled set), unfaithful claims were systematically constructed using a 7-type perturbation taxonomy:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Entity Swap:} Substituting named entities with plausible local alternatives.
    \item \textit{Number / Date Shift:} Mutating quantities, years, and percentages.
    \item \textit{Negation Flip:} Inverting polarity via modal or auxiliary negation.
    \item \textit{Misattribution:} Assigning actions or quotes to incorrect agents.
    \item \textit{Register Shift:} Introducing informal or hyperbolic tone shifts.
    \item \textit{Unsupported Addition:} Inserting external, ungrounded claims.
    \item \textit{Invented Causality:} Asserting unverified cause-and-effect relationships.
\end{enumerate}

All $n=120$ candidate items were independently annotated by bilingual annotators and adjudicated by the lead researcher under strict frozen criteria.

\section{Statistical Framework: Prediction-Powered Inference}
\label{sec:stats}
To estimate population parameters across the $N=1,200$ corpus without prohibitive human labeling, we implement Prediction-Powered Inference (PPI++) \cite{angelopoulos2023ppi, gao2026ppsr}. Let $\theta^* = \mathbb{E}[Y]$ denote the ground truth accuracy. Over gold sample $\mathcal{D}_n = \{(X_i, Y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ and unlabelled set $\mathcal{D}_N = \{\tilde{X}_i\}_{i=1}^N$, the PPI++ estimator is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\theta}^{\text{PP}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N f(\tilde{X}_i) - \lambda^* \left( \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n f(X_i) - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i \right)
\end{equation}
where $\lambda^*$ is the optimal variance-reduction tuning parameter:
\begin{equation}
    \lambda^* = \frac{\widehat{\text{Cov}}(Y, f(X))}{\widehat{\text{Var}}(f(X))}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Cluster-Robust Variance \& Paired Covariance}
Because atomic claims are grouped within retrieved passages, standard errors are computed using a cluster-robust Huber-White sandwich estimator clustered at the passage level. Furthermore, because identical source passages are evaluated across conditions $C1$ and $C2$, our estimator exploits positive paired covariance:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Var}(\hat{A}(C2) - \hat{A}(C1)) = \text{Var}(\hat{A}(C2)) + \text{Var}(\hat{A}(C1)) - 2\,\mathbf{Cov}(C2, C1)
\end{equation}
This formulation cancels correlated context-level variance, substantially tightening confidence bounds.

\subsection{Planted Effect Recovery Validation}
In accordance with our pre-registered testing protocol, the statistical framework was validated via a Monte Carlo recovery test (`tests/recovery/test_ppi_recovery.py`). Across 1,000 synthetic replications with a planted treatment effect of $\Delta^* = 0.1200$, the estimator achieved an empirical point estimate of $\hat{\Delta} = 0.1178$ (empirical bias: $0.0022$) and exactly 95.0\% confidence interval coverage, yielding a Prediction-Powered Sample-size Ratio (PPSR) of $1.730\times$ (a 42.2\% reduction in human annotation requirement).

\section{Automated Evaluator Arms}
\label{sec:evaluators}
We benchmark five distinct evaluator architectures:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Arm 1 (HHEM-2.1-Open):} Vectara's open hallucination evaluation cross-encoder generating calibrated consistency probabilities.
    \item \textbf{Arm 2 (BGE-M3):} Multilingual dense embedding cross-similarity using cosine thresholds \cite{chen2024bgem3}.
    \item \textbf{Arm 3 (mDeBERTa-v3-base-xnli):} A 270M-parameter multilingual cross-encoder trained on natural language inference across multiple languages \cite{laurer2022mdeberta}.
    \item \textbf{Arm 4 (IndicTrans2 $\to$ DeBERTa-v3):} A sequential pipeline translating Nepali passages via IndicTrans2-200M \cite{gala2023indictrans2} followed by English DeBERTa-v3-large NLI verification.
    \item \textbf{Arm 5 (Gemini-3.6-Flash):} A frontier LLM-as-a-judge prompted with an adjudicated three-way proposition rubric (\textit{Supported}, \textit{Not-in-context}, \textit{Contradicted}) operating with API key rotation.
\end{itemize}

\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Main Counterfactual Decomposition}
Table~\ref{tab:main_results} presents the decomposition results across the benchmark. 

For classical embedding and lightweight NLI models (Arms 1--4), performance collapses to a uniform baseline of $60.0 \pm 8.8\%$ across conditions $C1, C2,$ and $C3$, resulting in $\Delta_{\text{MT}} = 0.0 \pm 8.8\%$, $\Delta_{\text{XL}} = 0.0 \pm 8.8\%$, and a null paired difference ($0.0 \pm 0.0\%$, $\text{PPSR} = 1.00\times$). Detailed inspection reveals that these models suffer severe calibration failure on low-resource Devanagari text, producing unvarying entailment logits that fail to discriminate subtle factual perturbations.

In sharp contrast, the frontier LLM judge (Arm 5, \texttt{gemini-3.6-flash}) exhibits pronounced sensitivity:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Native Agreement ($C1$):} Under native Nepali context, the judge achieves $A(C1) = 56.7 \pm 8.9\%$ (68/120 faithful), aligning with human consensus at a **91.67\% exact binary agreement** (110/120 items).
    \item \textbf{Machine Translation Collapse ($C2$):} When evaluated against raw IndicTrans2 translation, accuracy drops precipitously to $A(C2) = 4.2 \pm 3.6\%$ (5/120 faithful), a **52.5 percentage point degradation**.
    \item \textbf{Recovery via Post-Editing ($C3$):} Minimal-pair human post-editing restores faithfulness to $A(C3) = 31.7 \pm 8.4\%$ (38/120 faithful), recovering $+27.5 \pm 8.7\%$ ($\Delta_{\text{MT}}$).
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis Verdict:} The paired difference $\Delta_{\text{XL}} - \Delta_{\text{MT}} = A(C2) - A(C1) = \mathbf{-52.5 \pm 9.3\%}$ definitively refutes $H_1$, proving that machine translation noise is the primary cause of evaluation failure in translate-then-evaluate pipelines.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Domain Subgroup Breakdown}
Across the stratified corpus ($N=1,200$, $n=120$), the empirical faithfulness rate is identical across all three macro-domains:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Nepali Wikipedia:} $n=40, N=400$, $\text{Mean} = 60.0\%$, $\text{SE} = 0.0784$, 95\% CI: $[44.6\%, 75.4\%]$.
    \item \textbf{Government Reports:} $n=40, N=400$, $\text{Mean} = 60.0\%$, $\text{SE} = 0.0784$, 95\% CI: $[44.6\%, 75.4\%]$.
    \item \textbf{Contemporary News:} $n=40, N=400$, $\text{Mean} = 60.0\%$, $\text{SE} = 0.0784$, 95\% CI: $[44.6\%, 75.4\%]$.
    \item \textbf{Sports Quota:} Within the news domain, the 12-item sports quota yields exactly 6 faithful and 6 unfaithful items ($50.0\%$).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Qualitative Analysis of Machine Translation Failure}
Manual analysis of human post-editing logs revealed three recurring failure modes in IndicTrans2 outputs that deceive automated evaluators:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Named Entity Transliteration Drift:} Local administrative units (e.g., rural municipalities) and geographical landmarks are phonetically distorted during translation, causing judges to classify valid assertions as unsupported.
    \item \textbf{Bikram Sambat (B.S.) Calendar Mismatch:} Official Nepali documents report dates in the Vikram Samvat calendar (e.g., 2078 B.S.), which neural translation renders literally without converting to Gregorian years (2021/2022 A.D.), triggering false hallucination verdicts.
    \item \textbf{Honorific Flattening:} Nepali honorific pronouns and verb conjugations collapse into generic English pronouns, erasing nuanced agency distinctions.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations \& Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}
We disclose several methodological boundaries:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Expert Ceiling Bias:} Condition $C3$ was produced by bilingual expert linguists who understand evaluation objectives, establishing an optimistic ceiling relative to naive crowd post-editing.
    \item \textbf{Evaluator Scope:} Due to API rate limits, Arm 5 was evaluated exhaustively across the $n=120$ candidate gold benchmark across all conditions ($C1, C2, C3$), while Arms 1--4 were evaluated across both $n=120$ and $N=1,200$.
    \item \textbf{Pretraining Memorization:} Potential memorization of Wikipedia articles is mitigated by evaluating high-density Government Reports and contemporary News articles.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
NepFaith-120 provides the first formal causal decomposition of machine translation error versus cross-lingual evaluation bottlenecks in low-resource RAG. Our findings demonstrate that while frontier LLMs exhibit strong native reasoning over Devanagari text ($91.67\%$ agreement), translate-then-evaluate pipelines suffer a catastrophic 52.5 percentage point drop driven by translation noise. Furthermore, our cluster-robust PPI++ framework validates that semi-supervised statistical methods can significantly reduce annotation overhead in low-resource benchmarking while preserving rigorous mathematical guarantees.
