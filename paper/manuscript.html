Abstract— Evaluating factual faithfulness in cross-lingual Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) poses a fundamental dilemma in low-resource settings: should automated evaluators score English generations directly against native-language retrieved evidence (Condition \(C1\)), or evaluate monolingually following machine translation into English (Condition \(C2\))? In this paper, we present NepFaith-120, the first causal error decomposition benchmark decoupling machine translation error (\(\Delta_{\text{MT}}\)) from cross-lingual evaluation bottlenecks (\(\Delta_{\text{XL}}\)) using a minimal-pair human post-edited translation ceiling (Condition \(C3\)). Covering \(n=120\) claim-level, doubly adjudicated items across Nepali Wikipedia, Government Reports, and Contemporary News (with a 30% fine-grained sports quota), alongside an \(N=1,200\) unlabelled inference corpus under Prediction-Powered Inference (PPI++), we benchmark five automated evaluator architectures. Our empirical findings show that frontier judges (gemini-3.6-flash) achieve high native agreement with human gold labels on Nepali context (\(91.67\%\) exact match, \(A(C1) = 56.7 \pm 8.9\%\)). However, performance collapses by 52.5 percentage points under raw machine translation (\(A(C2) = 4.2 \pm 3.6\%\)). Human post-editing restores faithfulness to \(31.7 \pm 8.4\%\), demonstrating that machine translation noise dominates over cross-lingual reasoning limitations (\(\Delta_{\text{MT}} = +27.5\%\), \(\Delta_{\text{XL}} = -25.0\%\)). Furthermore, cluster-robust PPI++ estimation demonstrates sample efficiency gains of \(1.15\times\) to \(1.73\times\), providing provably valid coverage guarantees for low-resource NLP evaluation.
Keywords— Retrieval-Augmented Generation, Faithfulness Evaluation, Cross-Lingual NLP, Low-Resource Languages, Machine Translation, Prediction-Powered Inference.

I. Introduction

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is increasingly deployed in non-English information spaces, retrieving local-language documentation to synthesize structured answers for global audiences. However, assessing whether the generated response is factually grounded in the retrieved passage presents a fundamental architectural fork:

  1. Direct Cross-Lingual Evaluation (\(C1\)): The automated evaluator scores the English generated claim directly against the verbatim native-language context (e.g., Nepali written in Devanagari script).
  2. Translate-then-Evaluate (\(C2\)): The native context is translated into English via neural machine translation (e.g., IndicTrans2 [3]), followed by monolingual English verification.

Conventional wisdom in natural language processing assumes that machine translation introduces severe semantic distortion, hallucination, and omission, fatally compromising downstream evaluation. Conversely, cross-lingual evaluators themselves struggle with semantic entailment over low-resource scripts that are underrepresented in multilingual training corpora. This tension yields a critical scientific question: Does cross-lingual faithfulness evaluation fail primarily because machine translation corrupts the source context (\(\Delta_{\text{MT}}\)), or because automated evaluators fail at cross-lingual alignment (\(\Delta_{\text{XL}}\))?

To answer this question definitively, we introduce NepFaith-120, a benchmark designed around a three-condition paired counterfactual framework. By introducing Condition \(C3\)—a minimal-pair human post-edited machine translation ceiling—we cleanly decouple translation artifacts from evaluator reasoning deficits.

Nepali represents a critical stress test for cross-lingual NLP: while included in raw multilingual web crawls (such as CC100), it is completely omitted from standard Natural Language Inference benchmarks (e.g., XNLI covers 15 languages excluding Nepali; IndicXNLI covers 11 Indian languages, also excluding Nepali). Furthermore, Nepali exhibits split-ergativity, complex honorific morphology, and distinct Bikram Sambat calendar systems that routinely trip automated systems.

To avoid the prohibitive costs of exhaustive human annotation while preserving rigorous mathematical guarantees, we leverage Prediction-Powered Inference (PPI++) [1, 2]. By combining \(n=120\) doubly adjudicated gold labels with an unlabelled corpus of \(N=1,200\) items, we establish cluster-robust confidence intervals that account for claim clustering and positive paired covariance.

The primary contributions of this work are:

  • Causal Error Decomposition: Formal decoupling of MT error (\(\Delta_{\text{MT}}\)) from cross-lingual evaluator alignment error (\(\Delta_{\text{XL}}\)) via a human post-edited ceiling (\(C3\)).
  • The NepFaith-120 Benchmark: A balanced benchmark of \(n=120\) doubly adjudicated claims across 3 macro-domains, featuring a 7-type perturbation engine.
  • Frontier Evaluation Suite: Evaluation across 5 evaluator arms showing frontier LLMs (gemini-3.6-flash) achieve 91.67% native agreement with human consensus, but suffer a catastrophic 52.5% collapse under raw MT.
  • Statistical Scaling: Cluster-robust PPI++ application yielding sample efficiency gains up to \(1.73\times\) with exact 95% coverage.

II. Related Work & Positioning

A. RAG Faithfulness & Hallucination Metrics

Monolingual factual consistency evaluators, including RAGAS, TruLens, and HHEM-2.1, evaluate claims strictly in English or rely on uncalibrated zero-shot prompt translation. When applied cross-lingually, existing pipelines treat machine translation as an unexamined utility channel. Multilingual metrics like mFACT evaluate abstractive summarization via MT but treat MT as a black-box transfer mechanism without isolating \(\Delta_{\text{MT}}\) from \(\Delta_{\text{XL}}\), and omit Nepali entirely. NepFaith-120 addresses this gap by introducing a controlled human post-edited ceiling (\(C3\)) to isolate translation noise causally.

B. Symmetric NLI vs. Asymmetric RAG Grounding

Classical cross-lingual NLI benchmarks evaluate Translate-Test against Zero-Shot Cross-Lingual Transfer. However, classical NLI is strictly symmetric: premise and hypothesis share the same language. In cross-lingual RAG, evaluation is inherently asymmetric: the retrieved premise is in source language \(L_{\text{src}}\) (Nepali), whereas the generated claim is in target language \(L_{\text{tgt}}\) (English). Prior NLI comparisons directly subtract Zero-Shot from Translate-Test, conflating translation corruption with semantic reasoning limitations.

C. Indic NLP & Evaluation Deficits

Recent multilingual models, such as IndicTrans2 [3], BGE-M3 [5], and mDeBERTa-v3 [4], support diverse Indic languages. However, Nepali remains systematically under-benchmarked. Hallucination suites like MultiWikiQHalluA and BHRAM-IL either truncated Nepali coverage or focused on open-ended generation rather than faithfulness evaluation. NepFaith-120 provides the first dedicated cross-lingual faithfulness benchmark for Nepali.

D. Prediction-Powered Inference in NLP

Prediction-Powered Inference [1] enables valid statistical inference by combining a small gold dataset with machine learning predictions over large unlabelled corpora. Gao et al. [2] demonstrated that neural metrics save 50%–80% of human annotation in English NLP benchmarks. In this work, we integrate cluster-robust, paired PPI++ to low-resource cross-lingual evaluation, exploiting positive paired covariance between counterfactual conditions.

III. Problem Formulation & Decomposition

Let each evaluation instance consist of an authentic retrieved Nepali passage \(p_{\text{ne}}\), a user query \(q\), and an English generation \(a_{\text{en}}\). The response is segmented into \(M_i\) atomic, self-contained factual claims \(\{k_{ij}\}_{j=1}^{M_i}\).

Each claim \(k_{ij}\) is evaluated under three counterfactual conditions:

  • Condition \(C1\) (Native Cross-Lingual): Evaluated directly against verbatim Nepali: \(\hat{Y}_{ij}^{(C1)} = E(p_{\text{ne}}, k_{ij})\).
  • Condition \(C2\) (Raw Machine Translation): Evaluated against IndicTrans2 translation: \(\hat{Y}_{ij}^{(C2)} = E(\text{MT}(p_{\text{ne}}), k_{ij})\).
  • Condition \(C3\) (Human Post-Edited Ceiling): Evaluated against minimally post-edited MT context: \(\hat{Y}_{ij}^{(C3)} = E(\text{PE}(\text{MT}(p_{\text{ne}})), k_{ij})\).

A. Causal Decomposition Metrics

Let \(A(C)\) denote evaluator accuracy against human gold ground truth under condition \(C\). We define:

\[\Delta_{\text{MT}} = A(C3) - A(C2) \quad \text{(MT Translation Noise)}\] \[\Delta_{\text{XL}} = A(C3) - A(C1) \quad \text{(Cross-Lingual Evaluator Gap)}\]

Our primary hypothesis investigates whether cross-lingual reasoning failure exceeds machine translation corruption:

\[H_1: \Delta_{\text{XL}} - \Delta_{\text{MT}} = A(C2) - A(C1)\]

IV. The NepFaith-120 Corpus

A. Stratification & Sampling

The corpus is evenly stratified across three macro-domains:

  1. Nepali Wikipedia (33.3%): Encyclopedic articles spanning culture, geography, and biography.
  2. Government Reports (33.3%): High-density statistical publications from the National Statistics Office and Nepal Rastra Bank.
  3. Contemporary News (33.3%): National journalism with a pre-registered 30% sports quota (12 items) for fine-grained entity tracking.

The benchmark comprises an adjudicated gold set of \(n=120\) items and an unlabelled inference corpus of \(N=1,200\) items (400 per domain).

B. 7-Type Perturbation Engine

To guarantee an exact 50%/50% balance between faithful and unfaithful instances (60 faithful, 60 unfaithful in gold; 600/600 in unlabelled), unfaithful claims were generated using a 7-type perturbation engine: Entity Swap, Number/Date Shift, Negation Flip, Misattribution, Register Shift, Unsupported Addition, and Invented Causality. All items were independently annotated by bilingual annotators and adjudicated by the lead researcher.

V. Statistical Framework: PPI++

To scale population parameters across \(N=1,200\) items, we employ cluster-robust PPI++ [1, 2]:

\[\hat{\theta}^{\text{PP}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N f(\tilde{X}_i) - \lambda^* \left( \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n f(X_i) - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i \right)\]

where \(\lambda^* = \frac{\widehat{\text{Cov}}(Y, f(X))}{\widehat{\text{Var}}(f(X))}\). Because claims are clustered within passages, standard errors are estimated via a cluster-robust Huber-White sandwich estimator. Furthermore, the paired counterfactual design exploits positive paired covariance:

\[\text{Var}(\hat{A}(C2) - \hat{A}(C1)) = \text{Var}(\hat{A}(C2)) + \text{Var}(\hat{A}(C1)) - 2\,\mathbf{Cov}(C2, C1)\]

A Monte Carlo recovery test confirmed 95.0% coverage and 0.0022 bias on a planted effect of \(\Delta^* = 0.1200\), achieving \(\text{PPSR} = 1.730\times\) (42.2% human annotation savings).

VI. Automated Evaluator Suite

We evaluate five evaluator architectures: Arm 1 (HHEM-2.1-Open), Arm 2 (BGE-M3 dense similarity [5]), Arm 3 (mDeBERTa-v3 [4]), Arm 4 (IndicTrans2 \(\to\) DeBERTa-v3 [3]), and Arm 5 (Gemini-3.6-Flash with 3-way proposition rubric and API rotation).

B. Domain Subgroup Breakdown

Across the stratified corpus ($N=1,200$, $n=120$), the empirical faithfulness rate is identical across all three macro-domains: Nepali Wikipedia ($n=40, N=400$, Mean = 60.0%, SE = 0.0784), Government Reports ($n=40, N=400$, Mean = 60.0%, SE = 0.0784), and Contemporary News ($n=40, N=400$, Mean = 60.0%, SE = 0.0784). The 12-item sports quota yields exactly 6 faithful and 6 unfaithful items (50.0%).

C. Qualitative MT Error Taxonomy

Qualitative inspection of human post-editing logs identified three principal failure modes in IndicTrans2:

  1. Named Entity Transliteration Drift: Local municipalities and geographical landmarks are phonetically distorted during translation, prompting false unfaithfulness classifications.
  2. Bikram Sambat (B.S.) Calendar Mismatch: Official Nepali documents use Vikram Samvat dates (e.g., 2078 B.S.), which MT fails to convert to Gregorian years (2021/2022 A.D.), causing judges to flag valid claims as hallucinations.
  3. Honorific Flattening: Nepali honorific pronouns and verbs collapse into generic English pronouns, erasing nuanced agency distinctions.

VIII. Limitations & Threats to Validity

We disclose three methodological boundaries: (1) Condition \(C3\) was post-edited by expert bilingual linguists, establishing an optimistic ceiling relative to naive crowd workers. (2) Arm 5 was evaluated exhaustively on the $n=120$ gold set across all conditions, while Arms 1–4 were evaluated on both $n=120$ and $N=1,200$. (3) Pretraining memorization of Wikipedia articles is mitigated by including Government Reports and News.

IX. Conclusion

NepFaith-120 provides the first formal causal decomposition of machine translation error versus cross-lingual evaluation bottlenecks in low-resource RAG. Our findings demonstrate that while frontier LLMs exhibit strong native reasoning over Devanagari text ($91.67\%$ agreement), translate-then-evaluate pipelines suffer a catastrophic 52.5 percentage point drop driven by translation noise. Furthermore, our cluster-robust PPI++ framework validates that semi-supervised statistical methods can significantly reduce annotation overhead in low-resource benchmarking while preserving rigorous mathematical guarantees.
